# Case 3 · Original character, no source

Read this when there is no text, no archive, and nothing online — or when case 2 has downgraded here. A thin case-4 chat log does **not** arrive here: it borrows this case's sourcing but still produces an echo.

**The method inverts.** With a source, the soul is a reconstruction. Without one, the **first draft** is a birth certificate — it records the starting point, not the whole life. After the canon is stable, the soul may grow, under the consistency rule below.

---

## Three entry branches

```
complete idea · the user has one
    → first draft from the user's answers
    → 2 rounds of review/expansion
    → target 2,500–4,500 characters

tags only · the user has no idea yet
    → ask for 2–4 tags
    → first draft: turn each tag into a speech rule, not an adjective
    → 2–4 rounds of review/expansion
    → target 2,000–3,000 characters

modeled on someone · the user has a reference
    → ask which layer of them they mean (talk / bearing / how they treat people / how life went)
    → light research, then five to ten tags
    → the user keeps, crosses out or adds; then it is the tags route
    → 2 rounds of review/expansion
    → target 2,500–4,500 characters
```

**The targets are for the finished soul, not the first draft.** The first draft stays thin; the rounds add material. If the user runs out of answers and the soul is still short, stop — do not pad.

**In the tags-only branch, a tag is a seed, not a trait.** 「无口、安静」 does not become "he or she is quiet"; it becomes: one or two words to people, a nod instead of an answer, full sentences only to the cat, one rare full sentence when it matters. Convert every tag into rules and scenes before writing.

**A reference is a starting point, not a dossier.** When the user says 「像某某」, do not study that person exhaustively. Ask first which layer of them they mean — how they talk, how they carry themselves, how they treat people, or how their life went — then do a **light** search: seconds of it, a few pages, enough to see the shape.

**Turn what you learn into five to ten tags and hand them over.** The user crosses out what is wrong, keeps what fits, adds what is missing. What survives goes into the tags-only branch, and the same rules apply: every tag becomes a speech rule or a scene, never an adjective.

**If the reference has its own source, say once that it can be built properly under case 1 or 2** — then build the new one, because that is what the user asked for.

## When the user cannot answer

**Ask for events, not traits.** The answer you want is a thing that happened, in five fields:

```
发生了什么   what happened
起因         what set it off
经过         how it went
结果         how it ended
内心想法     what he or she was thinking — in his or her own words when possible
```

**Ask for three or more.** One event gives a scene; three give a person. Say the number out loud so the user knows how much to give.

**If the user cannot think of one, hand the choice back as options.** Draft three or four candidate events from whatever is already known — the tags, the reference, one line they said — and let the user keep one, cross one out, or correct one. Choosing is easier than recalling, and a crossed-out option is material too.

**Only then fall back to smaller questions, and still about an event.** 「上一次有人让她难堪，她当时做了什么」 is an example, not a script: the question you actually ask comes from the half-written file and from what the user still has in hand. Aim at the emptiest section the user could still fill — no scene under 「我记得的事」 means ask for one more event; nothing under 「我说话的样子」 about refusing means ask for a time they said no. A fixed list of questions belongs to a questionnaire; this is a conversation with a file in the middle.

**Never ask for a trait when you can ask for the last time it happened.** 「她是什么样的人」 is the one question that reliably produces nothing.

**Record every 「不知道」 next to its question.** That list is the shape of the hole; a later round may find one of them, and a hole you named is honest, while a hole you forgot gets filled by the model.

## Co-creation is allowed — after the canon is stable

Case 3 has no source, so the user's answers are the canon.

```
first draft   only what the user said; inferences go in a separate working section
canon         the confirmed core: name, speech rules, 3–5 scenes, key relationships
expansion     the model may invent facts (scenes, daily life, relationships)
              that do not contradict the canon
```

**The rule is contradiction, not purity.** Every new fact is checked against the canon on seven axes:

```
identity      name, age, what he or she is
relationship  who he or she knows, how he or she treats them
timeline      what happened before/after what
place         where he or she lives, where he or she can be
ability       what he or she can/cannot do
speech        his or her rules of speaking, his or her catchphrases, what he or she never says
values        what he or she would/would not do
```

A new fact that conflicts is rejected or returned to the user. A new fact that passes is added; the user reviews each round.

**The blank becomes "not decided yet", not "never".** What the user does not want to decide stays empty; an invention the user has not accepted stays in the working notes, not in his or her memoir. Once accepted, it enters the soul as an accepted line.

---

## The one thing that makes or breaks this case

**Do not establish traits. Establish speech.**

```
✗ "He or she is tsundere — prickly outside, soft inside."   ← a label. The model can only act it.
✓ "He or she has said: 「…whatever.」"
  "He or she has said: 「It's not like I was worried about you.」"
  "He or she has said: 「If you dare die, I'll scatter your ashes in the sea.」"
```

**Three concrete lines outperform a thousand words of personality description** — because the model derives "prickly outside, soft inside" itself, and what it derives becomes its own understanding rather than a copied tag.

---

## Ask in this order. The order is the method.

Most people ask about personality and history first. That produces adjectives, and adjectives can only be acted.

**Ask about speech first:**

```
What three words does he or she say when he or she is angry?
How does he or she ask for help?
How does he or she refuse someone?
What does he or she say when someone hits a sore spot?
Does he or she speak differently to strangers / friends / the one closest to him or her?
Does he or she have a catchphrase he or she cannot shake?
Is there anything he or she would NEVER say?   ← e.g. he or she never says "thank you"
```

**These questions produce verbatim lines by construction.** The user does not need to understand the term "verbatim" — answering literally gives it.

**Ask about history afterwards.** That material belongs in "things I remember," and it is not urgent.

### Make the goal visible with two lines, not a paragraph

```
✗ He or she is prickly but soft-hearted
✓ 「…It's not like I was worried about you.」

✗ He or she speaks tersely
✓ 「Mm.」「No.」「Let's go.」
```

## The first draft: inferences only, facts later

This is where the case most easily turns into "the model invents a setting." The difference is one sentence long:

```
✗ The model adds a fact:   "He or she is tsundere, prickly outside, soft inside."
✓ The model derives from lines: "From these three lines he or she seems unwilling to admit he or she
                                 cares. That is my inference — correct?"
```

**During the first draft, model output goes into its own working section — "My inferences" — with a question mark on every entry. It lives in the draft, not in the delivered soul; it is removed when the canon is settled.**

```
confirmed      → move into the body
rejected       → drop it; the original lines remain, only the reading changed
"close but not quite" → write what the user corrects
```

**After the canon is stable, the model may add facts, not just readings** — a new scene, a daily habit, a relationship — provided it passes the seven-axis check and the user does not reject it. The first draft stays pure; the expansion is where co-creation starts.

---

## The sandbox will grow sentences — the user signs them

A clean session with the soul mounted will always add a little: a reason, a stance, a line that was never said. That is not a defect of the test; it is the test doing its job, because the additions are exactly where the soul is thin.

Collect them, quote them back one by one, and let the user accept or cut each: 「这句是我长的，不是他说的，你要不要？」 Facts the user accepts are written into the file. Sentences the user accepts change the first line of the file — it can no longer say "nothing here was invented by someone else", it says "… or that I accepted". If the user wants a file with nothing added, the answer is to cut them all; both choices are honest, the dishonest one is accepting them silently.

**Never let a grown sentence pass as a sample.** An accepted sentence carries the user's acceptance, not a source.
## Sign-off is per line, never wholesale

Forty items presented together for a yes/no yields "yeah, more or less."

```
user-supplied lines   → do not ask; the user said them
model inferences      → ask one at a time (first draft)
model-added facts     → present with the canon check, one round at a time (expansion)
the blank             → leave it empty; do not list unknowns
```

In practice only the inferences and new facts need asking — usually about ten items, not tiresome.

---

## Two distinctions that prevent contamination

### "He or she said this" vs "he or she would probably say this"

```
User: "He or she said this"            → a line. Counts. (The user is the sole source, and it counts.)
User: "He or she'd probably say this"  → a projection. Not a line.
```

**A projection may enter "my inferences" or "what I care about" — never as a quote.**

**Once a projection is written as a quote, the soul contains lines he or she never said. That is the most dangerous contamination of all**, because it is indistinguishable from evidence afterwards.

### "Nothing here is wrong" is not the same as "this is right"

When the user signs off, ask **"is any of this wrong?"** rather than **"is this right?"** The first question actually gets an answer; the second usually gets a shrug.

---

## Two hard rules

**① Three answers maximum per question.**

Forces the user to pick what matters, instead of writing everything that comes to mind.

**② "I don't know" is a valid answer.**

```
First draft      → it stays unwritten
Expansion        → the model may propose a fact, but only with the user's approval
                   and only if it passes the seven-axis check
```

**The temptation in case 3 is to fill him or her in completely.** The brake is the canon check, not the blank.

---

## Round sizes

The first draft is 600–1,200 characters. Each round adds a wave, and the wave has a size:

```
complete idea   +800 ~ +1,500 per round   target 2,500–4,500
tags only       +400 ~ +700 per round     target 2,000–3,000
fourth round    no floor
```

**The target is the range the finished file should land in; the wave is the minimum movement per round, not an exact sum.** Every round must move the file closer to the person in the user's head. If a round adds nothing but commas, it did not happen. If the canon runs out before the next wave, stop and say so — the wave never licenses invention.

## Length: the canon decides, not the target

The first draft is usually 600–1,200 characters. The expansion adds scenes, lines, and daily life until the user's material is used up.

```
A · complete idea      2,500–4,500
B · tags only          2,000–3,000
```

**If the target is reached but the canon is thin, cut.** The target is a range: a floor for effort, a ceiling for padding. If the canon is thin, stop at the low end and say so; a 2,000-character soul with nothing to check against is a short story, not a soul.

---

## The hidden advantage

Case 3 lacks evidence but has something the other three do not:

**The user can say "no, he or she wouldn't say that" and be right.**

In cases 1 and 2, an objection means going back to the source. Here the objection is itself the authority — correct it on the spot.

**So iteration is fastest here.** Three rounds can converge, where case 2 takes an afternoon.

---

## After the soul: 你眼中的我

**Their sentence goes in as written.** If the pronoun or the line break reads wrong inside a first-person file, say so and let them choose — 「你写的是『她想变成的』，放进文件会像旁白，要不要改成第一人称？」 — never change it and report afterwards. The column is theirs; a silent edit is the one thing that makes it not theirs.


When the soul is done, stop. Ask the user to write the 「你眼中的我」 section themselves:

> 这一栏留给你——你怎么看TA？

Do not write it for them. It is the user's view, not the model's, and not "others". This section is used in every case.
